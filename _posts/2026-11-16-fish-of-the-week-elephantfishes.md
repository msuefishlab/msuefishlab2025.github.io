Dr. Jason Gallant is the guest on this week's episode of [Fish of the Week!](https://www.fws.gov/fish-of-the-week-podcast), the award-winning podcast from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, hosted by Katrina Liebich and Guy Eroh. The episode, **"Elephantfishes!"**, is a 45-minute tour of the mormyrids: the roughly 200 species of weakly electric fishes from Africa that our lab studies in the field in Gabon and in the lab at MSU.

## Highlights from the episode

- **Electric songs.** Mormyrids make pulses of electricity from an organ in the tail built of modified muscle cells, stacked like batteries in a flashlight ("or, if you're more snack-oriented, fig Newtons in a tray"). They use those pulses to sense objects around them (electrolocation) and to communicate, firing anywhere from 10 to 100 pulses per second. Each fish's waveform is about as unique as a fingerprint, and it tells other fish its species, its sex and even who it is.
- **Fast-forward evolution.** Species of *Paramormyrops* in Gabon look like "little brown fish," but they can be told apart by their electric signals. That makes mormyrids one of the most rapidly speciating groups of fishes on the planet: "they give even cichlids a run for their money."
- **Big brains.** If you scaled *Gnathonemus petersii*, the elephantnose fish, up to human size, its brain would be larger than ours, thanks to a hugely enlarged cerebellum that processes electric signals.
- **Why "elephant" fish?** The trunk-like chin of the elephantnose fish, the Schnauzenorgan, is packed with electroreceptors for probing the mud for insect larvae. *Campylomormyrus* species have long bony snouts, and watching them eat is "like watching somebody try to eat spaghetti with a straw."
- **Sperm without tails.** Mormyrids are the only vertebrates known to have sperm with no flagellum. We talk about how fertilization might work anyway, and about a gene linked to the human condition primary ciliary dyskinesia that is altered in every mormyrid genome we've looked at.
- **Life in the lab.** Our MSU colony has about 200 live electric fishes from 15 species. They like hiding in "hotels" built from PVC pipe, which makes us "a black hole for PVC scrap" for campus contractors. They are also picky eaters that will skip frozen-food days and hold out for live worms.
- **Fieldwork in Gabon.** Over the years we've deposited roughly 1,000 preserved specimens, mostly in the MSU Museum, where researchers anywhere can borrow them "like a book in a library." Mormyrids are also part of everyday life and food in Gabon. Many fishermen there are amused to hear we've come all that way for the small fish that, in the villages, are traditionally caught by women.

The episode also covers how Dr. Gallant got hooked on electric fish (an animal communication course he took as a pre-med student) and tips for aquarium keepers. It ends with his picks for future episodes, including the electric eel and flashlight fishes.

Fish of the Week! started in 2021 to mark 150 years of fisheries conservation at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and each episode features a different fish and its experts. The show won Best Podcast at the 2022 Golden Post Awards and a bronze Anthem Award in 2024. Thank you to Katrina and Guy for having us on!
